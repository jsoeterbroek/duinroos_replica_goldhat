The find, acquired via the art market in 1996 for the Museum of Prehistory and Early History of the National Museums in Berlin (Prussian Cultural Heritage Foundation), allegedly originates from an old Swiss collection assembled in the 1960s (APA 32, 2000). Its find spot—likely somewhere in southern Germany—and the circumstances of its discovery are unfortunately unknown and, despite intensive research, have not yet been determined.

The Berlin Gold Hat is the fourth of its kind from Bronze Age Old Europe and the only one preserved in its original, complete form. Despite the lack of provenance, an analysis of this "key find" yields significant insights for interpreting the small but exquisite group of gold hats of the "Schifferstadt type" (Fig. 15; cf. Fig. 9).

...structured into the apex, the roughly conical shaft (with a maximum diameter of 125 mm), the 100 mm high crown, and the narrow horizontal brim (52–55 mm wide). The average wall thickness is 0.06 mm. The shaft is horizontally structured by 16 strongly profiled embossed ridges and is separated from the crown by an 18 mm high, vertically pleated transition zone. A bronze band—approximately 10 mm high and measuring 203 mm by 175 mm in diameter—is located in the fold between the base of the crown and the brim. The transition to the slightly deformed horizontal brim (outer dimensions 307 mm by 295 mm)—where the decorative patterns appear concave when viewed from above—is marked by a pleat in the gold sheet. The edge of the brim contains a twisted square-section wire made of bronze or copper (now broken in several places) featuring a distinctive clasp, around which the gold sheet is folded upwards.

The weight of this exceptionally well-preserved archaeological find, including the bronze reinforcements, is only 490 g. According to 1998 microprobe analyses conducted by the Institute of Metallurgy at the Technical University of Berlin, the gold consists of an alloy containing 9.8% silver, 0.4% copper, and 0.11% tin (Josef Riederer, APA 32, 2000, p. 107). According to the latest analyses using synchrotron radiation conducted at the Federal Institute for Materials Research and Testing (BAM) in 2008, mean values ​​were calculated for measurements taken at various points on the gold hat: 90.68% gold, 8.69% silver, 0.37% copper, and 0.16% tin for the apex; and 91.82% gold, 7.81% silver, 0.23% copper, and 0.10% tin for the middle section of the shaft (Hermann Born et al., APA 41, 2009, p. 34). The apex of the hybrid conical hat is adorned with an eight-pointed star pattern set against a dotted background, positioned above a circumferential band of 22 small embossed discs featuring two concentric circles.

The shaft, cap, and brim are decorated with 18 horizontal zones, each featuring identical embossed patterns of convex discs—bearing two, three, five, or six concentric rings—arranged in a continuous, repeating pattern such that the discs rarely touch or overlap one another. In the two zones located on the brim, the patterns are convex on the underside (cf. Fig. 18). Instead of embossed discs, the fifth zone below the apex displays a double frieze of horizontal crescent shapes with central dots, as well as a row of eye-like motifs.

The shaft is articulated by raised bands featuring a notched-bead design and is separated from the cap by a vertically pleated collar. The transition from the cap to the brim is designed in a similar, though narrower, fashion. The shaft, cap, and brim are divided into three sections—each comprising seven zones—by distinctive bands; the two zones at the base of the shaft (above the cap) overlap with the five zones located on the brim and the cap itself (Figs. 16–19).

Despite its fragility, the gold object shows hardly any signs of damage. Thin brownish encrustations on the cap and brim—likely resulting from copper corrosion within the gold alloy—suggest that the shaft was cleaned and the piece reshaped at some point prior to its acquisition by the State Museums. This conclusion is drawn from two vertical compression folds along the shaft, a kink at the base of the cap, and, above all, stress cracks in the slightly deformed brim that developed during millennia of burial in the ground. There are no ancient repairs or modern additions; archaeological assessment and scientific analysis leave no doubt as to the object's authenticity.

The apex, the conical shaft, the helmet-like cap, and the brim are decorated across 21 horizontal zones—clearly demarcated by separating ridges or bands—using a total of 24 different punching tools (predominantly circular and annular bosses, as well as notched-ribbon roulettes; cf. Hermann Born). The arrangement ensures there is no overlap between the disc and circular motifs, and only rarely among the other ornaments. The primary decorative elements are eight distinct types of disc bosses of varying sizes, featuring two, three, five, or six concentric rings. Special patterns include dot-filled horizontal semicircles, an eye motif, and an eight-rayed star set against a dotted background (Fig. 21). The arrangement of patterns within the vertical sequence of decorative zones reveals a clear system underlying the ornamental structure (Fig. 22, 1–4).

The apex of the gold hat measures 6.1 cm in height and has a basal circumference of 16.6 cm. The smooth-surfaced eight-rayed star features a freehand outline, while the eight acute-angled triangles between its rays display regular dotting arranged in horizontal rows (Zone 1). Zone 2—a 0.85 cm wide band set off by simple notched ridges—is also considered part of the apex; it contains 22 circular punches featuring two rings (No. 6; see Fig. 21), a motif that otherwise appears only as an interstitial pattern alongside the six-ringed discs (No. 15) found in Zones 15, 18, and 19. (Fig. 22, Ia)

The next notched ridge marks a shift in the decorative rhythm. The next two decorative bands follow a similar design. Zone 5 is 2.45 cm wide and displays two horizontal rows: at the top, 19 horizontal crescent shapes with a central dot (No. 16), and beneath them, an equal number of almond-shaped eye symbols (No. 17). Zone 10 is laid out as a mirror image of the preceding one. While the width remains the same, the band of annulets (No. 7) is positioned at the top and the band of dot-circles (No. 4) at the bottom. The repeating pattern consists of 19 bosses with five circles (No. 14), with the radial interstices filled by annulets (No. 7).

The adjoining Zone 11, framed by bands of dots (No. 7), features a band of equal width containing 20 correspondingly large bosses with three circles (No. 12) and annulets (No. 7), similar to those in Zone 10.

The decorative band of Zone 12 has the same width as the three preceding ones; it corresponds to them except for the main pattern, which consists of 20 bossed discs featuring five circles (No. 14), separated from one another by small annulets (No. 7) in the interstices between the discs. The circumference of the shaft, measured at the notched collar below, is 37.1 cm (Fig. 22, 2b).

This pattern repeats with nearly identical dimensions in Zone 13, though there it features 20 large discs with three concentric rings (No. 12)—identical to those found in Zones 9 and 11. The same applies to Zone 14, with its 21 bosses framed by five circles (No. 14), which appear in a repeating pattern identical to that of Zones 10 and 12.

At 3.8 cm, Zone 15 is significantly wider than the preceding ones. Framed by two beaded bands (No. 4), the main ornament consists of 15 bosses featuring six concentric circles (No. 15), combined with double-circle dots (No. 6)—like those found in Zone 2—positioned in the radial interstices (Fig. 23, 3a).

The final—or lowest—zone (Zone 16) of the shaft is separated from the preceding section by the recurring notched-bead band, and from the vertically fluted transition to the cap by a collar composed of three notched beads. This decorative band, 1.9 cm in width, is bordered at the top by a beaded band (No. 4) and displays 19 shallow boss-discs featuring two rings (No. 13)—a motif that appears only in this zone within the decorative repertoire—along with circles (No. 7) in the radial interstices.

The cap itself is delimited at its upper junction by a narrow band featuring dotted circles (No. 5), set in relief against the vertical fluting. The subsequent zone (Zone 17) is 1.5 cm wide and displays 33 small bosses featuring two circles (No. 9); like the boss-discs in Zones 7 and 16 (Nos. 11 and 13), this motif appears only once in the decorative repertoire. The most striking decorations on the cap are zones 18 and 19, which are identical to zone 15. Separated from zone 17 and from each other by bands featuring a double row of dots, and with double-circle dots (No. 6) in the radial interstices, zone 18 contains 21 and zone 19 contains 23 embossed discs featuring six circles (No. 15) (Fig. 22, 3b).

The brim displays the patterns in negative relief. The junction at the fold connecting to the cap—reinforced by a bronze hoop—is outlined by a radial raised ring. Immediately adjoining this is zone 20, containing 54 small embossed discs (No. 8) framed by three circles; these were evidently pressed into the gold sheet from below only after the base of the brim had been radially folded. This pattern is identical to the one found at the top of the shaft in zone 3.

Separated by a narrow band of ring-and-dot motifs (No. 7), zone 21 follows with 47 bosses featuring five circles (No. 14); these were created using the same pattern punch as those in zones 10, 12, and 14 on the shaft. The ornamental scheme—structured into 19 zones (excluding the tip) by 18 bands of notched ridges and five bands featuring dots, dotted circles, double-circle dots, and ring-and-dot motifs—concludes with the diagonally ribbed rim of the brim, which encloses a twisted square-section bronze wire wrapped in the gold sheet (Fig. 22, 4). The canon of symbols and the sequence of the 19 zones (3 through 21) evidently follow a relatively complex system—possibly originating from the radiating pattern at the cone’s apex—that only partially aligns with the formal structural division of the object as a whole. The width of the decorative bands and the number and size of the disc symbols are clearly correlated with the shaft's circumference at any given point; notably, the alternating vertical sequence of bosses featuring three and five circles is strictly maintained up to Zone 14.

If one disregards the apex (Zone 2)—which displays a row of double-circle dots of a type otherwise found only as interstitial patterns in Zones 15, 18, and 19—as well as Zone 5 with its 38 "special symbols," the 418 bossed discs (Nos. 8–15) are distributed across 18 horizontal ornamental zones and comprise a total of 1,701 concentric rings (Fig. 23).
