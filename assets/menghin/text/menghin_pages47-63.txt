The Calendar Hypothesis
As demonstrated by the horizon-astronomy-based "solar observatories," the circular enclosure earthworks of the European Neolithic interpreted as calendar structures, and the famous "Nebra Sky Disk" from the Early Bronze Age, the codification of "time" played a vital role in prehistoric Europe. It can be assumed that generations of systematic observations tracking the cyclical rising and setting of the sun, moon, and stars led to culture-specific calendar concepts. These concepts regulated life within respective societies as well as intercultural communication.
Beyond counting individual days, the simplest method for codifying time was the "month": the reappearance of the moon from new crescent to new crescent. This synodic or phased month averages rounded 29 ½ days with its four... [text cuts off]

... visible phases—from the new crescent to the waxing half-moon, full moon, and waning half-moon to the new moon—each lasting approximately 7 days. This is the easiest cyclical window to observe in the night sky. Twelve repetitions of this cycle span a time period of approx. 354 (354 ⅓) days.
The solar year with its 365 ¼ days was made apparent to structured societies in prehistoric Europe (especially as seen in the solar-oriented Neolithic and Early Bronze Age "calendar structures") through the path of the sun. Its cardinal points were the winter and summer solstices, as well as the equinoxes in spring and autumn, which are highly significant in northern latitudes. These four points divided the year into roughly four equal periods: three lasting 91 days and one lasting 92 days.
From the speculative (and rounded) division of the 91 days of a quarter-year, 13 weeks of 7 days each or 52 weeks plus 1 ¼ days for the year would follow.
The artificial division of the solar year into 365 days—where a leap day had to be added every four years—stands in contrast to the 12 (solar) months of 30 and 31 days of the solar year, compared to the lunar year with (rounded) 354 days comprised of 12 synodic months. These synodic months presumably alternated between 29 and 30 days.
The primary problem in synchronizing the solar and lunar years—which created the necessity for a permanent, binding time division and thus a "calendar"—was that the lunar year is roughly 11 days shorter than the solar year. If one continuously counted 12 synodic months year after year, it led to rapid temporal shifts. Over longer periods, these shifts had to be compensated for by inserting intercalary (leap) months so that "time" did not fall into chaos.
Through mere observation of the celestial paths of the sun and moon, prehistoric observers established that the exact same configurations of the moon (for example, the appearance of the new crescent on the day of the spring equinox) only repeat every 19 years. This corresponds to:
* $(4 \times 19) = \mathbf{76\text{ quarter-years}}$
* $[(76 \times 91) = 6916 + (19 \times 1\ ¼) = \mathbf{6939\ ¾\text{ days}}]$
This 19-year solar cycle contains 228 solar months (19 × 12) or 235 synodic (lunar) months, meaning 19 solar years require 7 intercalary (leap) months to realign.
How the people of prehistoric Europe recorded and transmitted data based on generations of celestial observations is the subject of many numerical calculations and theories, which ultimately remain speculative. However, it is reasonable to assume that illiterate cultures developed advanced knowledge of celestial mechanics and calendars within specific elite "castes," passing it down orally over generations. This is exactly how Julius Caesar described it being done during the last century BCE by the Gallic Druids. Memory capacity and rote learning in ancient, and especially non-literate times, were certainly far more developed than today. In the Bronze Age, knowledge of the cosmic order seems to have been deliberately captured visually through the ornamentation of the Gold Hats.
Historical Calendars
A binding, standardized division of time is essential for the organized coexistence of complex societies. Ancient cultures developed vastly different calendar concepts based on observations of the sun, moon, and stars.
A. In Ancient Egypt, the annual cycle was determined by its three "seasons," which were dictated by cyclically recurring natural events like the Nile floods around the end of June (according to the Gregorian calendar). This approximate date coincided in the ancient Egyptian calendar with the heliacal rising of the fixed star Sirius (ancient Egyptian Sothis). This alignment could be observed with the naked eye even by farmers who possessed no advanced astronomical knowledge. The phases of the moon's cycle served to measure time and schedule religious festivals.
The fundamental problem with early timekeeping stems from the differing number of days between the average 354 days of the lunar year and the slightly less than 365 ¼ days of the solar year. The Egyptian "agricultural year" began with the first new moon following the Nile flood or the heliacal rising of Sothis. Because the lunar cycle lags behind the solar cycle by 11 days each year, an additional leap month was automatically added when needed. Consequently, the lunar or lunisolar year consisted of an irregularly alternating cycle of 12 and 13 months (lunations).
Based on this lunisolar year, an independent "administrative" calendar was introduced around 2700 BCE. This administrative calendar was completely decoupled from natural phenomena, featuring 360 days divided into 12 months of 30 days, plus five intercalary days. The three seasons each contained four months of 30 days. This abstract, simplified calendar lacked a leap year mechanism. As a result, like a clock without a leap year rule, the calendar slipped forward by one day every four years. It moved further and further away from natural cyclical anchor points, requiring 1460 years to return to its original starting point. Despite this limitation, this priesthood-sanctioned calendar remained in use in the temples of Egypt until the era of Augustus (according to: Lexikon der Ägyptologie, Vol. III (1980), S. 297–298).
B. The earliest advanced astronomy can be traced back to the cultures of the Ancient Near East. These calendars were determined by the lunar cycle and were likely tied closely to cultic rituals performed in daily, monthly, and annual cycles. The scheduling of most festivals hung directly on the behavior of the moon. They are... [text cuts off]

... Ancient Mesopotamian festivals often shared their names with the month in which they were celebrated. In addition, there were secondary occasions for celebrations that were directly linked to the seasons—and thus to the position of the sun—as indicated by month names derived from agricultural activities.
This "cultic calendar" united both astronomically determined cycles of the moon and the year by inserting intercalary (leap) months to ensure that the months of the lunar year always fell within the exact same seasons.
The annual solar cycle also appears to have been determined in Mesopotamia through horizon observations. Cuneiform texts from the 11th century BCE describe how the starting point of the sun shifts throughout the course of a year, and specify the exact months and days on which solstices and equinoxes ideally occur. The latter were calculated through the continuous monitoring of day and night lengths over the course of a year. The growing discrepancy between actual astronomical data and the schematically fixed cardinal points in a quartered circle of the solar path provided the cue for when an additional month needed to be intercalated.
The Babylonian lunisolar calendar can be reconstructed from numerous cuneiform texts dating from the 7th century BCE onward. As reflected by the month names—which are closely tied to the seasons and traditional cultic festivals—its development in Mesopotamia reaches far back into the 3rd millennium BCE of our timeline.
In Babylonia, the day began at sunset and lasted until the following sunset. A new month was initiated by the appearance of the crescent moon on the western horizon. This first visibility as a thin crescent (new light) marked the start of the month, and its recurrence as new light after a new moon determined the duration of the month, alternating between 29 ("hollow month") or 30 ("full month") days. The year was determined by these cyclically recurring periods, beginning with the spring month of Nisan (March/April according to the Gregorian calendar).
A "normal" lunar year has 354 days and is therefore roughly 11 days shorter than the solar year. Consequently, every third lunar year—i.e., between the 24th and 36th synodic month—an additional 13th month had to be intercalated to prevent the start of the year from drifting too far from its natural seasonal anchor point, such as the vernal equinox.
Despite the ability to track the orbital paths of the sun and moon (including its lunar nodes, the Saros cycle of 228 months spanning roughly 18 years, and the regularly recurring lunar eclipses) through systematic observations and centuries of written records, no binding, standardized intercalation rule can be verified in the Babylonian calendar until late historical periods. When an additional month was inserted and which years were to be leap years was decided on a case-by-case basis by the king, who was undoubtedly advised by his astrologers and star-gazers according to cuneiform texts.
These temporal fluctuations in the system meant that alongside the cultic calendars, an "administrative calendar" was also used in Mesopotamia since ancient times. In these systems, months were calculated with a standardized length of 30 days, independent of actual lunar phases, resulting in a consistent 360-day year.
Only from around 380 BCE onward can a regular 19-year cycle be verified in Babylonia, in which the 1st, 3rd, 6th, 9th, 11th, 14th, and 17th years were designated as leap years (source: Reallexikon der Assyriologie und Vorderasiatischen Archäologie, Vol. 1 (1976–1980), p. 297).
Astronomers in Mesopotamia developed a numerical theory for calendars quite early on. The parameters of Babylonian astronomers, derived from analyzing long-term observations, provided the foundation for Greek astronomers like Hipparchus (2nd century BCE) and Ptolemy (2nd century CE). However, whether the 19-year cycle was first computed by Meton in Athens or used much earlier in Mesopotamia remains a matter of debate. No hints of this specific intercalation scheme appear in cuneiform texts prior to the 11th century BCE. Yet, through analysis of astronomical cuneiform texts from around 750 BCE, John Britton suggests that intercalations based on the 19-year cycle (19 years equal 19 solar years plus 7 leap months of 30 days each) were strictly maintained from that period until the second regnal year of Xerxes (484 BCE).
C. The Jewish calendar is a lunisolar calendar tied to the phases of the moon utilizing intercalation rules based on the 19-year cycle, heavily influenced by Babylonian astronomy in its origins. It combines the lunar month with the solar year so that holidays always fall in the same seasons, generally on fixed calendar dates.
A month lasts from new moon to new moon, spanning 29 or 30 days. The "regular" (lunar) year has 354 days. Because it is roughly one-third of a day too short, every three years an "excessive" (abundant) lunar year consisting of 355 days is scheduled. In other years, a day is subtracted from a month ("deficient year"), ensuring that these corrective measures yield exact average monthly values when balanced against solar and lunar eclipses mapped over vast spans of time.
Alignment with the solar year is achieved via intercalations tracking the 19-year cycle. Additional (full) months of 30 days are inserted in the 3rd, 6th, 8th, 11th, 14th, 17th, and 19th years of the (lunar) cycle. According to the calendar rules of the Jewish year, these intercalations can be classified as "regular," "excessive," or "deficient."

Taking religious exceptions into account, the Jewish calendar can have six different year lengths. A common year (with an even number of full months) can have 353, 354, or 355 days. A leap year can have 383, 384, or 385 days. This distinguishes it from the rules for the regular solar and lunar years. Consequently, it does not just feature regular common and leap years, but also "deficient" (353/383 days), "regular" (354/384 days), and "excessive" (355/385 days) years.
Through these measures, the calendar remains optimally aligned with the paths of the sun and the seasons, ensuring that the average calendar year, at 365.24 days, is nearly identical to the numerical value of the tropical year.
The Jewish New Year begins with the new moon of the 7th month, whereas the spring month of Nisan is designated as the 1st month in biblical tradition. This month corresponds to late September/early October (according to the Gregorian calendar) or late March/April.
D. As in all ancient cultures, archaic Greece struggled for centuries to create a usable calendar by unifying the solar year with the lunar year. Well known is the Delphic Oktaetris, the "Great Year," where an 8-year lunar period (8 x 354) was aligned with 8 solar years by adding three synodic months of 30 days. In this lunisolar calendar, five years had twelve months and three had thirteen months, which led to inaccuracies due to the cyclical progression of the moon.
Attempts to counter this problem by doubling the cycle yielded no real progress. Greek astronomers from the school of Euctemon in the 5th century—who developed a 19-year switching cycle based on long-term celestial observations and presumably with knowledge of Babylonian astronomy—were the first to achieve success. 19 solar years correspond numerically to 19 lunar years plus 7 months. Among the 235 synodic months, they designated 110 as "hollow months" with 29 days and 125 as "full months" with 30 days, resulting in 6,940 days (110 × 29) + (125 × 30) = 6940.
In this cycle, the alternating month values are correctly distributed and align properly with celestial phenomena. This enabled Meton of Athens to calculate the summer solstices for the year 432 BCE precisely and improve the calendar. He erected columns for astronomical observation and set up a pegboard calendar (parapegma).
Unlike the large empires of the Ancient Near East, the Greek city-states never managed to agree on a unified calendar despite all efforts. New Year dates were scattered throughout the entire year, particularly around the days of the cardinal points, though the spring equinox is rarely documented. The dating of the year was handled individually based on the assumption of office of the highest officials or priests of the main deity of the respective community. The leap month appeared at different points in the year, and the calendar systems varied widely. Proven similarities existed only between the calendars of Athens and Delphi. The start of the year was the summer solstice, and the 6th month was typically used for intercalation. From 330 BCE onward, Delphi also adopted the Metonic cycle. Otherwise, treaties between multiple Greek cities often had to be signed with three or more different date lines (Source: Paulys Realencyclopädie der Classischen Altertumswissenschaften 20 [1919] s.v. „Kalender“. – Ibid. 1. E. Before the introduction of the Julian calendar, a lunar calendar combined with a solar element was used in the Roman Republic. The normal year was calculated with 355 days, followed in a regular pattern by a leap year of 377 or 378 days. This resulted in a four-year cycle spanning a total length of 355 + 378 + 355 + 377 = 1465 days, creating an excess of four days compared to the duration of four tropical years with 1461 days.
The month lengths in a normal year with 355 days were calculated as 7 × 29 and 4 × 31, and in the 2nd month (Februarius), 28 days. In leap years, the length of February was only 23 days, and an additional month (Intercalaris) of 27 or 28 days was appended. The first day of each month was called the Kalendae, the 5th day in months with 31 days was the Nonae, and the 13th, 15th, or 17th day of the month was the Idus.
Because this calendar system was one day too long per year and additionally suffered from the manipulation of inserting leap months with 27/28 days, alignment with the moon phases was lost. The priests (Pontifices) set the leap months arbitrarily, which led to calendar chaos without astronomical regulation. Prior to the introduction of the Julian calendar in 46 BCE, the republican calendar was roughly 90 days ahead of the seasons despite multiple reform attempts.
The calendar developed by the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes on behalf of Julius Caesar is a pure solar calendar in which the moon phases are completely ignored. Following the tradition of the ancient Egyptian calendar, it features 12 months tied to the zodiac and is correlated with the seasons, as the year is determined by the path of the sun between two cardinal points, typically the spring equinox. It spans 365 ¼ days, meaning every four years a leap year with 366 days must be scheduled, so that the four-year cycle contains 1461 days.
When scheduling "movable" feasts tied to the phases of the moon—such as Easter and its associated holidays—the solar Julian calendar still had to rely on a lunar calendar and its tracking adjustments.
